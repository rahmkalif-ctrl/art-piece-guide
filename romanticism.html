Romanticism & Landscape

The Disembarkation of Cleopatra at Tarsus

Artist: Claude Lorrain (1642) — Baroque Classical Landscape

Artist Context & Historical Analysis: French landscape innovator Claude Lorrain captures the legendary arrival of Egyptian Queen Cleopatra in Asia Minor to meet Mark Antony. Lorrain uses low horizon lines, delicate atmospheric hazes, and golden solar illumination to transform a historic encounter into an idyllic, idyllic seascape. His pioneering approach to rendering natural sunlight reflecting off harbor ripples established foundational aesthetic ideals for 18th and 19th-century Romantic landscape painters throughout Europe.

Pandemonium

Artist: John Martin (1825) — British Sublime Romanticism

Artist Context & Historical Analysis: Renowned for apocalyptic visions, John Martin depicts Satan summoning fallen angels outside the infernal palace of Pandemonium, inspired by John Milton’s epic poem Paradise Lost. Martin constructs a colossal subterranean cityscape bathed in fiery magma hues, juxtaposing miniature demonic armies against overwhelming fiery architecture. The composition embodies the Romantic aesthetic of the "Sublime"—an overwhelming combination of awe, terror, and cosmic grandeur that fascinated viewers during the rise of the Industrial Age.

The Last of the Buffalo

Artist: Albert Bierstadt (1888) — Hudson River School

Artist Context & Historical Analysis: German-American painter Albert Bierstadt presents a poignant, monumental tribute to the vanishing American wilderness and decimation of North American bison herds in the late 19th century. Through luminous atmospheric perspective and dramatic topography, Bierstadt contrasts Indigenous hunters with scattered bison remains in the foreground. The canvas serves as both a Romantic eulogy for the pristine West and a critical commentary on rapid industrial expansion and ecological destruction.

The Raft of the Medusa

Artist: Théodore Géricault (1819) — French Romantic Masterpiece

Artist Context & Historical Analysis: Géricault shocked the 1819 Paris Salon with this monumental depiction of the notorious 1816 shipwreck of the French frigate Méduse, where 147 survivors were abandoned on a makeshift raft for 13 days. Arranging emaciated corpses and despairing figures into a surging upward pyramid, Géricault channels intense emotional raw drama, fleshly realism, and political outrage against royal naval incompetence, establishing Romanticism as a powerful force for social critique.

The Bullfight

Artist: Francisco Goya (1824) — Spanish Romanticism

Artist Context & Historical Analysis: Created during his exile in Bordeaux, Spanish master Francisco Goya captures the violent energy and raw spectacle of traditional Spanish bullfighting. Utilizing thick, expressive impasto brushwork, earthy tonal palettes, and dynamic movement, Goya focuses on primal human-animal conflict and crowd psychology rather than idealized classical grace, prefiguring modern expressionist techniques through his visceral emotional realism.
